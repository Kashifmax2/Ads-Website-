Is your phone really fast-charging? Three ways to check

Almost every charger sold today has a big number printed on it. Most phones never see that number, because fast charging is a negotiation, not a setting — and the weakest part of the chain decides the result.

By the TechSarthi desk9 November 20268 min read

What "fast charging" actually means

Charging power is volts multiplied by amps. A phone and a charger agree on a voltage and a current limit before power starts flowing, so a 20W charger might deliver 9V at 2.2A, while a 65W laptop charger might sit at 20V at 3.25A. The phone asks for what it can safely accept; the charger offers what it can supply. If the two do not share a protocol, they fall back to the slowest safe option — usually 5V at 1A or 5V at 2A.

There are only a few things a phone can speak:

  • USB Power Delivery (USB PD) — the common standard, used by iPhones from the 8 onward, most Android flagships and nearly every laptop. PD 3.0 tops out at 100W; PD 3.1 with Extended Power Range reaches 240W over the right cable.
  • USB PD PPS — a PD variant that adjusts voltage in small steps. Samsung's Super Fast Charging and Google's fast charging rely on this.
  • Proprietary standards — SuperVOOC, Warp Charge, Xiaomi's HyperCharge and similar. These need the brand's own charger and often the brand's own cable.
  • Quick Charge — older Qualcomm versions used their own signalling; from Quick Charge 4 it is built on top of USB PD.
The important bit A charger, a cable and a phone all have to agree. Buying a 100W brick does not make a phone that only accepts 18W charge any faster, and a cheap cable can pull a 65W charger back down to 15W.

Three checks that take a minute each

1. Read what the phone says

Many Android phones print the result on the lock screen: Charging, Fast charging, Super fast charging or Super fast charging 2.0. If it says only "Charging", you are on the slow fallback and there is nothing more to measure.

iPhones do not show a rate. The lock screen says "Charging" whether you are on a 5W brick or a 30W PD charger, so for an iPhone you need one of the next two tests.

2. Time a 30-minute charge

This is the most honest test, because it measures what actually reached the battery.

  1. Use the phone until it is around 20%. Do not start below 10% or above 40% — every phone slows down at the top end.
  2. Plug in with the screen off, in a cool room, and leave it alone for exactly 30 minutes.
  3. Note the percentage gained. Then repeat the next day with the other charger and cable, starting from a similar percentage.

As a rough guide on a recent phone, a 20W PD charger adds roughly 35–50% in half an hour. A 5W charger adds about 8–12%. If a charger labelled 45W adds the same as an old 10W brick, the negotiation is failing — usually because of the cable.

3. Use an inline USB power meter

A small meter that sits between the cable and the phone costs about as much as a mid-range cable and shows live volts, amps and watts. It is the only way to see the number rather than guess it.

Watch the reading for the first minute. A phone in fast-charge should climb to its negotiated voltage and hold a steady wattage. Readings that flicker between 5W and 25W, or sit at 5V the whole time, mean the charger and phone never agreed. A meter also tells you whether a laptop charger's second port is stealing power: on many multi-port bricks, plugging in a second device drops the first port from 65W to 30W or less.

Why a "fast" charger can still trickle

  • The cable is the usual suspect. Standard USB-C cables are rated for 3A. Above that you need a 5A or e-marked cable, and cheap long cables drop voltage over their length. Swap the cable before you buy anything else.
  • Pocket lint in the port. A compacted layer of lint stops the connector seating fully. A wooden toothpick or a plastic pick can lift it out; never use metal.
  • Heat. Phones cut the charge rate sharply once the battery passes roughly 38–40°C. Charging under a pillow, in a case, or while running a game will slow it down more than any charger choice.
  • The battery is nearly full. Above about 80%, every phone tapers off deliberately. That is not a fault.
  • The screen is on. Some phones split the incoming power between the screen and the battery, so a phone in active use charges at a fraction of its rating.
  • The charger only speaks one proprietary standard. A SuperVOOC brick will treat a Samsung phone as an ordinary 10W charger.

What we do on our own phones

We keep one good USB PD charger per room and one spare 5A cable in the bag. The phone's own cable lives with the phone, because a cable that has been coiled around a power bank for a year is usually the reason charging got slow. If a phone charges slowly on a known-good charger and cable, we clean the port and try again before assuming the battery is failing.

One safety note: fast charging is safe when the charger is certified, and risky when it is not. An unbranded 100W brick that costs less than a branded 20W one has no reason to contain the protection circuitry it claims. A charger that gets too hot to hold is not a bargain.
