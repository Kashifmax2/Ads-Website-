Selling an old Android phone? Do these eight things first

A factory reset is not a security step on its own. Done in the wrong order it leaves your Google account attached to the phone — and the buyer cannot get past the setup screen. The whole checklist takes about twenty minutes.

By the TechSarthi desk6 November 20267 min read

1. Back up what you want to keep

Photos, call recordings, WhatsApp chats and two-factor authenticator codes are the four things people discover they have lost after the sale. Google's own backup covers app data and settings; it does not cover everything.

  • Copy photos and videos to a computer with a USB cable, then confirm the files open.
  • Export important WhatsApp threads from inside the app (chat → three dots → Export chat), with media.
  • Move authenticator codes to a new device before wiping. If the codes were only on the old phone, sign in to each account now and re-enrol.

2. Sign out of Google — and remove the account

This is the step that causes the most trouble. Android's Factory Reset Protection remembers the last Google account on the device. After a reset, the phone asks for that account's password, and a buyer who does not have it is stuck with a phone they cannot set up.

Go to Settings → Accounts (or Passwords & accounts), open your Google account and choose Remove account. Do the same for Samsung, Xiaomi, Huawei or any other brand account, and for any work or school profile. Removing the account while you are signed in is what disables the protection cleanly.

The step people skip Signing out is not the same as removing. If the account still appears in Settings → Accounts, the phone will ask for it after the reset. Check that the list is empty.

3. Turn off Find My Device and anti-theft

Google's Find My Device and Samsung's SmartThings Find can both block a reset or re-lock a phone remotely. Turn them off before wiping: Settings → Google → Find My Device, and on Samsung, Settings → Security and privacy → Find My Mobile. Also switch off any third-party anti-theft app you installed.

4. Take out the SIM and memory card

A SIM left inside is a working phone number in someone else's hands. A microSD card can hold years of photos that a factory reset will never touch, because the reset only covers internal storage on most phones. Eject both, and keep the card if it still has value; otherwise destroy it.

5. Encrypt, then factory reset

Every Android phone from version 6 onward encrypts internal storage by default. On an older device, encrypt first: Settings → Security → Encrypt phone. Then reset from Settings → System → Reset options → Erase all data.

Do not reset from recovery mode instead. A recovery wipe can leave the account protection and the encryption keys in an inconsistent state, and it is harder to prove the wipe completed.

6. Check and note the IMEI

Dial *#06# before you reset, and photograph the screen next to the phone's box. The number should match the sticker on the box and the tray. A buyer will usually check it against a stolen-phone database; so should you, because a phone reported stolen by a previous owner cannot be activated.

Keep the IMEI written down after the sale. If the buyer later claims the phone was faulty, you have a record of exactly which handset left your hands.

7. Clean the hardware properly

Wipe the screen with a dry microfibre cloth, and clear the charging port with a wooden toothpick — pocket lint is the most common reason a perfectly good phone is sold as "charging fault". Clean the camera glass and the speaker grille. If you are selling with a case, wash the case separately; a cracked case makes a good phone look abused.

8. Meet safely and take payment first

Meet in a public place during the day, or use a platform with buyer protection. Let the buyer test calls, the camera and charging, but keep the phone until the money has cleared — a bank transfer screenshot is not a payment. If you sell to a shop, ask for a written receipt with the IMEI.

What buyers check first

  • Battery health. Most Android phones do not show a battery percentage, so buyers look for a third-party reading or ask how long it lasts. If it is below about 80%, say so before the meeting.
  • Screen burn and dead pixels. Open a full white image and a full grey one.
  • Water damage. The tray or the SIM slot often has a small indicator that changes colour.
  • Account lock. The setup screen should ask for a Wi-Fi network, not for a password.

If the sale falls through

Do not hand the phone back to a stranger and assume it is clean. If someone has set it up with their own account, reset it again and remove their account first — otherwise the next buyer inherits the lock. This is also why it is worth doing the whole checklist in one sitting, with the phone in front of you.

The short version

  • Back up photos, chats and authenticator codes
  • Remove every account, not just sign out
  • Turn off Find My Device
  • Remove the SIM and memory card
  • Factory reset from Settings
  • Note the IMEI before it goes
