Why your Wi-Fi drops at the same time every evening

A network that is fine all day and unusable at nine at night is not usually broken. The pattern is the clue: something changes at that hour, and most of the time it is not your router.

By the TechSarthi desk2 November 20269 min read

Two causes, one symptom

Evening slowdowns almost always come from one of two places. Either the air around you gets crowded when the whole street starts streaming, or your internet provider's neighbourhood link gets saturated at peak hours. They look identical from the sofa, and they need completely different fixes — which is why the first job is to find out which one you have.

The ten-minute test

Run this at the bad time, not at three in the afternoon. A speed test taken when everything works proves nothing.

1. Compare wired and wireless

If you can, plug a laptop into the router with an Ethernet cable and run a speed test. If the wired connection is also slow, your Wi-Fi is innocent: the problem is the line, the router's routing, or the provider. If the wired connection is fast and the phone is slow, it is a wireless problem and you can fix it at home.

2. Ping the router, then the internet

On a phone or laptop, ping the router's address (often 192.168.1.1) for a minute, then ping a public address such as 1.1.1.1. High latency or loss to the router means the wireless link is struggling. Low latency to the router but high latency to the internet means the link beyond your flat is the problem.

3. Look at the channel

A free Wi-Fi analyser app shows every network around you and which channel it uses. If your 2.4GHz network shares a channel with four or five neighbours, you have found the cause. This is normal in flats and terraced housing, and it gets worse in the evening when those networks are busy.

4. Try 5GHz

If your router broadcasts a 5GHz network, connect to it and repeat the test. 5GHz has far more channels and much less interference, at the cost of range. If 5GHz is stable and 2.4GHz is not, you do not need a new router — you need to move your devices across.

Do the test twice Once at the bad hour and once at a quiet hour. The difference between the two runs tells you more than either run alone.

The usual suspects, in order

  • 2.4GHz congestion. The band has three usable, non-overlapping channels: 1, 6 and 11. Most routers ship on one of them and most neighbours never change it. At peak hours the airtime is shared between everyone on your channel, and every network slows down.
  • Household interference. Microwave ovens, baby monitors, wireless doorbells, Bluetooth speakers and some cordless phones all sit on or near 2.4GHz. A microwave running at dinner time can knock out the band for the length of a reheated plate.
  • Provider peak-hour congestion. Residential links are shared. When the street comes home and starts streaming, the shared segment fills up. Nothing in your flat fixes this, but the wired test in step 1 is how you prove it before you buy hardware.
  • Router heat and load. Cheap routers run hot, and some throttle after hours of continuous load. Feel the case at the bad time; if it is uncomfortably hot, give it air and see whether the drops move.
  • The DHCP lease. Many routers hand out addresses for exactly 24 hours. If the lease renews at the same time each evening, devices can stall for a few seconds — usually a blip, not a full evening of slowness.
  • Power saving on the device. Laptops and phones drop the wireless link aggressively to save battery. A laptop that disconnects at idle but reconnects the moment you type is doing this, not failing.
  • Old firmware. Router firmware from three years ago has known bugs in band steering and roaming, and some of them only show up under load.

Fixes, cheapest first

  1. Move the router. Off the floor, out of a cupboard, away from the TV, the microwave and any metal. Central and high beats hidden and tidy.
  2. Put your devices on 5GHz. Keep 2.4GHz for printers, plugs and other low-demand gadgets. If both bands share one name, rename them so you can choose.
  3. Change the 2.4GHz channel to whichever of 1, 6 or 11 is least used near you, and set the width to 20MHz. Narrower is slower on paper but far more reliable in a crowded building.
  4. Update the firmware and reboot on a schedule. A reboot once a week hides a lot of small faults while you plan a proper fix.
  5. Lengthen the DHCP lease to a week if your router allows it, which removes the daily renewal blip.
  6. Turn on QoS for video calls if the router has it. It will not add bandwidth, but it stops one download from ruining a meeting.
  7. Add a wired access point rather than a repeater. A second access point on a cable is the only fix that reliably adds coverage without halving speed.

When a new router is actually worth it

Replace the router if it only speaks 2.4GHz, if it is more than seven or eight years old, or if it drops connections under load even when wired. If it supports 5GHz and holds a wired connection at full speed, a new router will mostly buy you better range — and a mesh kit or a wired access point usually buys more.

If the wired test shows the line itself slowing at peak hours, the useful call is to your provider, with the numbers in front of you: the time, the wired speed, the wireless speed and the ping. That is a support ticket they can act on, rather than "the internet is slow".

Quick checks

  • Wired slow too? It is the line, not the Wi-Fi
  • 2.4GHz crowded? Move devices to 5GHz
  • Channel shared? Use 1, 6 or 11 at 20MHz
  • Router hot? Give it air
  • Still bad when wired? Call the provider
